Who may invest

Any offering will be made only to persons who qualify as accredited investors under Regulation D of the Securities Act of 1933 and, where applicable, qualified purchasers under the Investment Company Act of 1940, and only in jurisdictions where such an offering is permitted. Interests may not be transferred or resold except as permitted under the fund’s governing documents and applicable law, and there is no public market for them and none is expected to develop.

No operating history

Trident is newly formed. It has no operating history, no assets under management, and no completed acquisitions. The fund described on this website has not yet held a closing. The figures on this website describing the screened book — the number of filings, how they sort into bands, what is refused and why, the lenders behind them, the county medians and Fair Market Rents — describe Trident’s screening process applied to public foreclosure calendars. They are not a record of properties acquired, rents collected or returns earned, and they are not indicative of future results. The screen can be applied consistently and still produce poor outcomes.

Distributions are not guaranteed

Distributions to investors depend on the fund acquiring properties, leasing them, and collecting rent in excess of operating costs and reserves, and on the terms set out in the memorandum. None of that is assured. An investor may receive no distributions for extended periods, may receive less than projected, and may lose some or all of the amount invested. Any yield figure on this website describes a screening threshold, not an expected or targeted return to investors.

Risk factors

The memorandum will contain a complete discussion of risks. Among the risks specific to this strategy:

  • Surviving liens. A sheriff’s deed conveys the property subject to liens the judgment did not extinguish, including senior mortgages, municipal charges, unpaid utilities, and federal tax liens with their redemption rights. Title searches may fail to identify every encumbrance.
  • Lien position. A filing may be a subordinate lien, association lien or private judgment rather than a first mortgage, in which case the senior debt survives the sale and the apparent discount does not exist.
  • Owner-occupant right of first refusal. Under New Jersey’s Community Wealth Preservation Program a foreclosed owner-occupant may purchase the property at the opening bid, displacing third-party bidders. Its application and procedures continue to develop.
  • Occupancy and possession. Properties may be occupied at purchase. New Jersey law restricts eviction to good cause and the process can be lengthy and costly. Obtaining possession may require payment to occupants.
  • Condition. There is no interior access before a sheriff’s sale. Properties are purchased as-is and may have defects, environmental conditions or damage that materially exceed make-ready estimates.
  • Adjournment and withdrawal. Sales are frequently adjourned, often without re-advertisement, or withdrawn when a borrower reinstates, refinances or files for bankruptcy. Capital may remain undeployed for extended periods, reducing returns.
  • Deposit forfeiture. A winning bidder who fails to pay the balance within the required period forfeits the deposit.
  • Valuation. County medians and assessor-derived values are estimates. Independent valuation may also be wrong. A property may be worth less than the screen assumed.
  • Voucher program dependence. The strategy depends on the Housing Choice Voucher program, which is federally funded and locally administered. Changes in funding, payment standards, inspection requirements or administration could reduce rents, delay leasing or make properties ineligible.
  • Make-ready and leasing. Properties must pass housing authority inspection before a voucher tenant may occupy them. Make-ready may cost more and take longer than estimated, during which the property produces no income.
  • Concentration. The fund will hold a limited number of properties in a limited number of counties in one state, and will be exposed to local economic, regulatory and legal conditions.
  • Illiquidity. Interests are not transferable except as permitted by the fund’s governing documents. There is no market for them. An investor should expect to hold for the full term of the fund.
  • Key persons. The strategy depends on two principals. The loss or unavailability of either could materially affect the fund.
  • Regulatory and legal change. Foreclosure procedure, tenant protection, the Community Wealth Preservation Program and the voucher program are all subject to legislative, regulatory and judicial change.
  • Conflicts of interest. Trident and its principals will receive compensation from the fund as set out in the memorandum, will invest in the fund, may have interests in other ventures, and may face conflicts in allocating time and opportunities. The memorandum describes these.
  • Leverage. The base structure uses no acquisition debt. If the fund later borrows within the limits the memorandum permits, lenders would rank ahead of investors on the properties concerned.
  • Fees and expenses. The fund will bear management fees, organisational and operating expenses, and the manager’s participation in profits, all as set out in the memorandum. These reduce returns to investors.
